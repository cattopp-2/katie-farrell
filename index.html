Getting Started

How the skill system works and what's available to you.

Skills are pre-built instructions that tell Claude how to handle a specific type of task, in your voice, for your audience, using your context files.

When you run a skill, Claude reads the instructions for that task, loads the relevant context files (your voice profile, your audience language, your offer details), and produces output that fits your brand. It doesn't produce generic output from scratch. It works from your system.

Think of a skill like a trained staff member who already knows your business. You don't explain who your clients are every time, or what your tone of voice is, or how you like your emails structured. The skill already has all of that.

Skills also follow proven copy and content frameworks. A sales page skill doesn't just write words, it follows a specific structure built on conversion principles (because I've built them like that!).

An email sequence skill knows the right flow to take someone from first contact to ready to buy.

A social post skill uses frameworks that are designed to stop the scroll and get engagement.

You get the output of those frameworks without needing to follow a template. It's all built into the skills.

What skills are in your system

Your AI Hub comes with skills for writing blog posts, creating social content, building email sequences, writing sales pages, repurposing video, researching keywords, building audience language files, and more.

The full list is in the cheatsheet in this module.

You don't need to learn all of them before you start. You'll discover the relevant ones as you build your content rhythm.

How to Call a Skill

There are three ways to call a skill.

1
Slash commands
The first is a slash command. Some of the most-used skills have a shortcut. Just type it into the chat panel and press enter:
/content-router: takes an idea and writes it in whatever format you choose
/seo-blog-writer: writes a fully optimised blog post
/wrap-up: closes the session and saves what needs saving
2
Run by name
The second is running a skill by name. For skills without a slash command, type:
run [skill name]

For example: run story-researcher or run email-sequence-writer. Claude will load the skill, ask any questions it needs, and then do the work.
3
Describe what you want
The third is simply describing what you want.
You don't need to know which skill handles it. Say "write me a Facebook post about this" or "I need an email sequence for my new offer" and Claude will identify the right skill and run it.

The slash commands and run syntax are shortcuts for when you already know what you want.

How Skills Improve Over Time

After every skill run, the system asks if you have any feedback. If you do ("make the emails shorter", "skip the bullet lists", "don't open with a question"), that gets saved to a file called learnings file inside the skill folder. The next time you run that skill, it reads those notes first and applies them before it starts.

You never have to repeat yourself. The system gets better the more you use it.

At the end of every session, running wrap-up also checks whether anything from your conversation is worth saving to your permanent memory files: preferences, decisions, context that matters next time. Two minutes at the end of each session, and nothing gets lost.

End Every Session With Wrap-Up

The one thing to do before you close Claude every time.

Run this every time before you close Claude. It takes about two minutes and it's what makes the system compound over time.

Type at the end of every session:
/wrap-up

What it saves

The wrap-up skill writes three things:

memory/sessions/[date].md — today's session log. What you worked on, what decisions were made, what was produced.

memory/MEMORY.md — the persistent facts index. Key decisions, preferences, and context Claude should remember across all future sessions.

context/tomorrow.md — your open threads list. Anything left unfinished, any decisions still pending, any tasks carried forward.

What happens without it

Claude's built-in memory doesn't persist between sessions. Without wrap-up, the next session starts cold — no memory of what you decided, no open threads, no captured feedback. The system loses what it learned.

With wrap-up, every session builds on the last. This is the habit that makes the whole thing work.

CEO Check
ceo-check
Strategy
Before you say yes to a new idea, a collaboration, or a piece of content — run this. It checks the thing against your north star and tells you if it's aligned, off-track, or uncertain.
run ceo-check
"CEO check — someone's asked me to co-host a summit on women's leadership. Is this worth my time right now?"

What the verdicts mean

Aligned — this is a good use of your time right now. Do it.

Off-track — this pulls you away from your current priorities. Park it or decline.

Uncertain — there's a case either way. Claude flags what's unclear and suggests what would help you decide.

The habit that makes it useful

The skill is most valuable when you use it before you say yes. Before you commit to something, run a CEO check. It takes about 30 seconds and stops you spending a week on something that doesn't move the needle.

You can also run it on your full open threads list: "CEO check on my open threads" — Claude reads through your tomorrow.md and tells you what to focus on and what to drop.

Repurpose Audio & Video

Turn one recording into a full week of content — Facebook, LinkedIn, Instagram, and email.

This is your primary content workflow. You record a voice note or video — talking about something you're thinking about, a client situation, a pattern you've noticed — and Claude turns it into a full batch of platform-ready posts.

The whole process takes about five minutes of your time. Claude does the rest.

What you get from one recording

  • 2 Facebook posts (different angles and frameworks)
  • 1 short Facebook post + first comment (for engagement)
  • 2 LinkedIn posts
  • 1 Instagram caption
  • 1 Instagram carousel (slide copy)
  • 1 nurture email with subject line options

Everything lands in Airtable as a draft for you to review. Nothing gets posted without your sign-off.

Step 1 — Save your file

Save your audio or video file to this folder in Dropbox:

Folder:
brand-context/Videos and Audios to repurpose/

Any format works — .m4a, .mp3, .mp4, .mov. Name it something recognisable, like Energetic Triggers.m4a or Nervous system post.m4a. You don't need to tidy it up.

If you already have a written transcript or an .srt subtitle file, you can drop that in the same folder instead — Claude can read those directly and skip the transcription step.

Step 2 — Transcribe

Open a Claude Code session and say:

Type:
Transcribe [filename] — it's in brand-context/Videos and Audios to repurpose/

Claude will run the transcription tool and save a .md file in the same folder. The file will be named with the date and topic, like 2026-08-27-energetic-triggers.md.

Once it's saved, Claude will tell you it's done. You can read it back to check, but you don't need to — it goes straight into the next step.

If you already dropped in a transcript or SRT file, skip this step entirely.

Step 3 — Run the skill

Once the transcript file exists, say:

Type:
Repurpose this voice note — [brief description of the topic]

For example: "Repurpose this voice note — it's about how people get energetically triggered by others who are spiritually open."

The description helps Claude pick the right angles and offer connection. Keep it to one sentence.

What Claude does next

  • Reads the transcript and extracts the core insight and angles
  • Writes each piece using a different framework and angle — no repetition
  • Runs everything through the humaniser to remove AI tells
  • Saves files to projects/social-posts/[date]_[topic]/
  • Saves all 7 pieces to Airtable as Katie to check

Claude will ask you one question at most — usually about the CTA if it's not obvious from the recording. Otherwise it runs through without stopping.

Step 4 — Review in Airtable

When the skill finishes, your content is in Airtable. Open the Katie to check view and you'll see the new posts listed.

Airtable:
Katie's Content System → SM Content → Katie to check

Click into each record to read the post. If you're happy with it, move it to Finalised. If you want changes, drop a note in the record or come back to Claude and say what needs adjusting.

You don't need to review everything at once. The drafts sit there until you're ready.

Language Rules

Your voice profile captures how you sound. Language rules add a separate layer: what Claude is explicitly never allowed to write.

Without language rules, Claude defaults to patterns that are technically fine but feel off: em dashes, phrases like "here's the thing", structures like "it's not X, it's Y". These are so common in AI-generated content that they've become dead giveaways.

Run setup-language-rules

Type run setup-language-rules in the chat panel. The skill starts with a curated list of the most common AI writing offenders, asks you four quick questions to personalise it, then saves your file.

Your language rules save to brand-context/language-rules.md. Every content skill loads this file alongside your voice profile before writing anything. The rules are treated as hard constraints, not suggestions.

The ongoing habit

Every time Claude produces a phrase that jars — something that makes you think "I'd never say that" — tell it in the session and then say: "Add that to my language rules."

Claude opens the file, adds the phrase, confirms it's done. That phrase will never appear in your content again.

Over time, this file becomes one of the most valuable parts of your system. The more you refine it, the less editing you do.

The more you add over time, the more accurately the system can write for your specific audience rather than a generic version of them.

Your Visual Brand

Your brand guidelines file is the single source of truth for how everything visual looks — website pages, carousels, presentations, any design Claude builds for you.

Where it lives

Your visual brand guidelines are stored in:

Folder:
brand-context/Visual Brand Guidelines/

The key file is brand-guidelines.md. It contains your full colour palette (with HEX values), your typography system (heading, body, subheading, accent fonts and their Google Fonts fallbacks), CSS variables ready to drop into any build, your logo variants and when to use each one, and usage rules for colour and type.

How it gets used

When you ask Claude to build a page or a visual, it reads brand-guidelines.md first. That means every page starts from your navy, gold, and rose palette, your Playfair Display headings, and your EB Garamond body copy — not something generic. You don't describe your brand each time; it's already loaded.

Building a page

The typical workflow is:

  1. Write the copy — using service-page-writer, sales-page-writer, or your own draft
  2. Say: "Design a page for [offer] using this copy" — Claude reads your brand guidelines and builds the layout using viz-interface-design
  3. The output is an HTML file you paste into a Divi code block on your WordPress site

If your brand evolves

Update brand-guidelines.md — change a colour value, swap a font, update a rule. Every future build picks up the change automatically. The HTML version (brand-guidelines.html) is a visual reference you can open in a browser to see everything at a glance.

If you ever need to rebuild it

The guidelines were extracted from your brand PDF (Brand Guide.pdf in the same folder). If you ever need to rebuild the file from scratch — say, after a full rebrand — say to Claude: "Rebuild my brand guidelines from the PDF in brand-context/Visual Brand Guidelines/" and it will extract the colours, fonts, and rules and rewrite the file.

"The Facebook Post 1 from the Energetic Triggers batch needs reworking — the hook is too abstract. Can you try a more personal story angle instead?"

Claude will rewrite it and save the updated version back to the same record.

Work through one recording's batch at a time. Review, approve, schedule — then move on to the next. It's faster than dipping in and out.

Building a Website Page

Write the copy first, then ask Claude to build the HTML. The skill reads your brand guidelines automatically so every page matches your design system.

This is a two-step workflow. The copy and the design are separate jobs — getting them in the right order means you're never retrofitting words into a layout, or redesigning around copy that's going to change.

The two steps

  1. Write the copy — use a writer skill to draft it, review and approve it
  2. Build the HTML — run web-page-builder, which reads your brand guidelines and existing pages to produce consistent HTML ready for Divi

What web-page-builder does

  • Reads your brand guidelines (colours, fonts, CSS variables, usage rules)
  • Studies the existing pages to match the established patterns
  • Plans the section structure and waits for your approval before building
  • Produces a single self-contained HTML file saved to brand-context/Website/pages/
Step 1 — Write the Copy

Use the right writer skill for the page type:

  • Service page (Human Design, Energy Healing, Retreats) → service-page-writer
  • Sales / programme page (Her Evolution, group offer) → sales-page-writer
  • Short page (opt-in, thank you) → write it yourself or ask Claude to draft it directly

Say:
Run service-page-writer for my [offer name] page

The skill will ask you questions about the offer, the audience, and the keyword you want to target. Answer them one at a time. It will produce a full copy draft.

Before moving to Step 2

Read through the copy. Make any changes — add specifics, adjust the tone, swap out any lines that don't sound like you. Once you're happy with it, say: "The copy is approved — build the HTML."

Step 2 — Build the HTML

With the copy approved, say:

Say:
Build the HTML for this page using web-page-builder

The skill will:

  1. Read your brand guidelines and study the existing pages
  2. Plan the section layout and show you the structure — hero, content sections, CTA
  3. Wait for you to confirm the plan before building
  4. Produce the full HTML and save it to brand-context/Website/pages/[Page Name]/

Reviewing the output

Open the saved HTML file in your browser to preview it. You can ask Claude to adjust anything — spacing, section order, colours, copy tweaks — before it goes into WordPress.

Step 3 — Paste into Divi

Once you're happy with the preview:

  1. Open the HTML file and copy the entire contents
  2. In WordPress, open the page in Divi Builder
  3. Add a full-width section → insert a Code module
  4. Paste the HTML into the code module and save
  5. Preview the page in WordPress to check it renders correctly

Divi's own header and footer wrap the page automatically — the HTML Claude produces is just the page body content, so there's no duplication.

If something looks off

Come back to Claude and describe what's wrong: "The hero text is too small", "The CTA button isn't showing", "The spacing between sections is too tight." Claude will fix the HTML and you re-paste it.

The Folder Structure

What each folder in your system does and what lives inside it.

Context Folder

This is where Claude learns who you are and how you work.

It contains files like:

Everything that makes your content sound like you and speak to your audience.

What is Markdown?

How to open, read, and edit the files your system runs on.

Almost everything in the Business Brain OS is stored as a markdown file — a plain text file with a .md extension. Your brand voice, your audience language, your offer details, your daily memory, your context files: all markdown.

If you've never heard of markdown before, don't worry. You don't need to learn it properly. But knowing what it is and how to handle it will save you confusion later.

Why Markdown?

Claude reads plain text files natively and quickly. Markdown adds basic structure (headings, bullet points, bold text) without needing a word processor. The files are small, portable, and easy to version-control — which is how GitHub tracks updates to your skills without touching your personal files.

Most importantly: Claude can read, write, and edit markdown files directly inside your session. You don't need another app.

What it Looks Like

Markdown is just text with a few simple symbols. A # at the start of a line makes a heading. **text** makes it bold. A - at the start of a line makes a bullet point. Everything else is a normal paragraph.

That's 90% of what you'll ever see in your files. The rest Claude handles for you.

Opening and Editing Files

Inside Claude Code, click any .md file in the sidebar. It opens in a panel on the right. You can read it there, and you can ask Claude to make changes: "Update my north star file to add X" or "Add this to my writing samples."

You can also edit files directly. Click the pencil icon in the panel to switch from preview to edit mode, make your change, and save.

Outside Claude Code, any text editor will open a markdown file: Notepad on Windows, TextEdit on Mac, or VS Code if you have it. It's just text.

Never open a markdown file in Microsoft Word. Word adds invisible formatting that can break how Claude reads the file.

Getting Documents Into Your System

You'll sometimes want to bring in content from outside: a Google Doc, a PDF, an old email, notes from a call. Here's how.

  • If you can copy the text — paste it into the chat. Then ask Claude to save it: "Save this as a file in brand-context/writing-samples/ and call it email-example-1.md." Claude creates the file, formats it cleanly, and puts it in the right place.
  • For Google Docs — copy the text and paste it into the chat. The formatting won't come through cleanly, but a quick "tidy this up and save it" prompt fixes it in a few seconds.
  • For PDFs — either paste the text (if it's selectable) or drag the file into the Claude Code chat window. Claude can read PDFs directly and pull out what you need.

The Interface

When you open Claude Code, you'll see three main areas.

1. The Chat Panel

The middle of the screen — where your conversations with Claude take place. You type at the bottom prompt, press Enter, and Claude responds in the space above.

The context bar shows how much of the chat panel is being filled as the conversation grows. When it gets close to full, Claude starts to lose track of things said earlier in the session. Around 120K tokens is a good time to wrap up rather than pushing on.

Modes: Leave it on Auto for day-to-day use. Switch to Plan if you're about to ask Claude to do something big and want to review the steps first. Don't use Bypass permissions.

Models: Use Sonnet 4.6 as your default. Switch to Opus only if you're doing something that genuinely needs more reasoning power.

2. The Left Sidebar

Your conversation history. Every session you've had in Claude Code is listed here, most recent at the top. You can pin sessions, rename them, and group related ones into folders.

3. The Right-hand File Panel

Your window into the folder your system lives in. Click a file to open it in preview. Click the pencil icon to switch to edit mode.

Use it for: checking what's in a context file, making a quick edit, confirming Claude saved something correctly. For rewriting, reorganising, or updating skills — ask Claude to do that instead.

Permissions

Claude Code is not running in the background. It doesn't do anything unless you open a session and give it an instruction. When you close the app, nothing runs.

The Business Brain OS comes pre-configured with a permissions file that controls exactly what Claude is and isn't allowed to do.

What it's allowed to do

  • Read, edit, and write files inside your Business Brain OS folder
  • Run specific scripts that power the skills
  • Basic file navigation: listing folders, checking where things are

What it's blocked from doing

  • Installing software — blocked by default
  • Deleting files or folders permanently — blocked by default
  • Reading your credentials or .env files

Even if Claude misunderstood an instruction and tried to delete a folder, the system would stop it.

The mode selector controls how much Claude checks in with you before acting — not what it's capable of doing. The guardrails above apply regardless of which mode you're in.

Connecting External Tools

Left to itself, Claude can only work with files on your computer. It can't see your email, your Google Drive, your Airtable, or any other external tool — unless you connect it.

That's what MCP connections do. MCP stands for Model Context Protocol — the standard that lets Claude plug into external apps. Each connection is something you set up deliberately, and it only gives Claude access to that specific tool.

What becomes possible with MCP connections

  • Airtable — Claude can read your content queue, save posts, and update records directly
  • Google Drive — Claude can read documents from a link, without you copying and pasting
  • WordPress — Claude can publish blog posts as drafts straight from a session

If a connection isn't set up, Claude simply can't access that tool. And the connections only work within the scope of what you've configured.

Your Memory System

Every time you open Claude Code in your Business Brain OS folder, the first thing it does is read your CLAUDE.md file. From there, it reads the relevant files depending on what you've asked it to do.

Your open threads list

Your open threads list is saved in a file called tomorrow.md that carries forward between sessions. Anything left open at the end of a session gets saved here by the wrap-up skill. When you open a new session, Claude reads this file and picks up where you left off.

The context window

You'll notice a progress bar at the top of the chat panel. This is the context window — the total amount of conversation Claude can hold in memory at once. Around 120K tokens is a good point to either wrap up or use /compact.

/compact

When you're in the middle of something and don't want to stop, type /compact and save to working memory what we're working on. Claude compresses the conversation history and saves a summary so it doesn't lose the thread.

The wrap-up skill

At the end of every session, type /wrap-up. It writes to three files:

  • memory/sessions/[date].md — today's session log
  • memory/MEMORY.md — the persistent facts index
  • context/tomorrow.md — your open threads list

Without running wrap-up, you risk losing all the learnings and context from that session. This is the habit that makes the whole system compound over time.
